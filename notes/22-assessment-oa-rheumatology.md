# Assessment of Osteoarthritis and Rheumatological Conditions

> Source: David J. Magee, *Orthopedic Physical Assessment*, 6th ed., and companion MSK assessment material. Paper 3 compilation.

## ASSESSMENT OSTEOARTHRITIS (OA) OF THE KNEE

1. Subjective Assessment: Pain History: - Understand the patient's experience of knee pain, including its location, intensity, frequency, and aggravating/alleviating factors. - Assess whether the pain is localized to a specific area or radiates to other parts of the leg. Functional Limitations: - Identify how knee pain affects the patient's daily activities, such as walking, climbing stairs, getting up from a chair, and standing. - Ask about any modifications the patient has made to their activities due to knee pain. Medical History: - Gather information about the patient's medical history, including any past injuries, surgeries, or medical conditions that may influence their knee OA.

- Determine if the patient has any coexisting health conditions that could impact their ability to participate in physiotherapy interventions. 2. Objective Assessment: Observation: - Observe the patient's gait (walking pattern) for any noticeable abnormalities, such as limping, favoring one leg, or an antalgic gait due to pain. - Look for swelling, muscle atrophy, and joint deformities in the knee region. Palpation: - Gently palpate around the knee joint to identify areas of tenderness, swelling, and warmth. - Check for the presence of bony growths (osteophytes) along the joint margins. Range of Motion (ROM): - Measure the active and passive range of motion of the knee joint to assess for any limitations or pain during movement.

- Evaluate both flexion (bending) and extension (straightening) of the knee. Muscle Strength Testing: - Evaluate the strength of muscles surrounding the knee joint, including the quadriceps, hamstrings, and calf muscles. - Muscle weakness can contribute to functional limitations and altered joint mechanics. Functional Testing: - Assess the patient's ability to perform functional tasks, such as squatting, rising from a chair, and stair climbing. - Observe for compensatory movements and any pain during these activities. Special Tests: - Perform specific tests to assess ligament stability, joint laxity, and functional capacity of the knee. - For instance, assess the patient's ability to balance on one leg or perform a single- leg squat. 3.

Assessment of Functional Limitations: Timed Up and Go (TUG) Test: - Measure the time it takes for the patient to stand up from a chair, walk a short distance, turn around, and sit back down. - This test assesses the patient's mobility and balance during a common daily task. 4. Patient-Specific Assessment:

## Functional Questionnaires

- Use standardized questionnaires such as the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) or Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) to quantify pain, stiffness, and physical function. 5. Communication and Education: Patient Education: - Explain the findings of the assessment to the patient in an easily understandable manner. - Discuss the goals of physiotherapy, potential treatment options, and the importance of adherence to the treatment plan.6. Treatment Planning: Exercise Prescription: - Develop an individualized exercise program that includes range of motion exercises, muscle strengthening, and flexibility exercises.

- Focus on improving quadriceps and hamstring strength, as strong muscles can help stabilize the knee joint. Pain Management: - Discuss pain management strategies, such as ice application, pain-relieving modalities, and activity modifications. Gait Training: - Address any gait abnormalities and provide guidance on improving walking patterns to reduce strain on the knee joint. Functional Training: - Incorporate functional exercises that mimic the patient's daily activities to improve overall function and confidence. Home Exercise Program: - Provide the patient with a home exercise program that they can perform independently between physiotherapy sessions.

## ASSESSMENT OF HIP OSTEOARTHRITIS (OA)

1. Subjective Assessment: Pain History: - Explore the onset, duration, and progression of hip pain. Understand the nature of the pain (aching, sharp, throbbing) and its specific location. - Ask about pain triggers, aggravating factors (e.g., specific activities, positions), and alleviating factors (e.g., rest, heat, pain medications). Functional Limitations: - Assess how hip pain impacts daily activities, including walking, sitting, standing, stair climbing, and getting in/out of a car. - Determine if the patient has made any activity modifications due to hip pain. Medical History: - Gather information about previous hip injuries, surgeries, or medical conditions that might contribute to or influence hip OA. - Note any relevant comorbidities that could impact the treatment approach. 2. Objective Assessment:

## Observation

- Observe the patient's gait for deviations, such as a limp or antalgic gait pattern (walking with a shortened stride to avoid pain). - Note any visible deformities, muscle wasting, or swelling around the hip joint. Palpation: - Palpate the hip joint, hip flexors, hip extensors, adductors, and abductors to identify tender areas, muscle tension, and bony prominences. - Check for any joint crepitus or irregularities. Range of Motion (ROM): - Assess active and passive ROM of the hip joint, including flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, internal rotation, and external rotation. - Compare with the non-affected side and note any limitations, discomfort, or pain.

Muscle Strength Testing: - Evaluate muscle strength of the hip muscles, particularly the hip abductors, adductors, flexors, extensors, and external rotators. - Weakness in these muscle groups can contribute to altered joint mechanics. Functional Testing: - Assess the patient's ability to perform functional tasks such as squatting, stepping, and standing on one leg. - Observe for pain, limitations, compensatory movements, and any difficulties. Special Tests: - Perform specific tests to assess joint stability, ligament integrity, and hip range of motion. - Consider using the Trendelenburg test to assess hip abductor strength and function. 3.

Functional Limitation Assessment: Timed Up and Go (TUG) Test: - Measure the time taken by the patient to stand up from a chair, walk a short distance, turn around, and sit back down. - This test evaluates mobility and balance during a common functional task. 4. Patient-Specific Assessment: Functional Questionnaires: - Use standardized questionnaires such as the Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (HOOS) or the Oxford Hip Score to quantify pain, stiffness, and physical function. 5. Communication and Education: Patient Education: - Explain the assessment findings clearly, addressing the patient's concerns and questions. - Provide information about the role of physiotherapy, the available treatment options, and the importance of compliance.

6. Treatment Planning: Exercise Prescription: - Develop an individualized exercise program that includes mobility exercises, muscle strengthening, and flexibility exercises. - Focus on improving hip stability and overall function. Pain Management: - Discuss pain management strategies, including the use of ice, heat, pain-relieving modalities, and ergonomic adjustments. Gait Training: - Address gait abnormalities and provide guidance on optimizing walking patterns to minimize hip joint strain. Functional Training: - Incorporate functional exercises that simulate the patient's daily activities, promoting overall functional improvement and confidence.

Home Exercise Program: - Provide the patient with a tailored home exercise program, ensuring they understand how to perform the exercises correctly. 7. Regular Re-assessment: - Periodically re-evaluate the patient's progress to track improvements and make necessary adjustments to the treatment plan. - Collaborate with the patient and other healthcare professionals as needed for comprehensive care.

## ASSESSMENT OSTEOARTHRITIS (OA) OF THE HAND

1. Subjective Assessment: Pain History: - Explore the nature of hand pain, including its characteristics, duration, and any patterns of exacerbation or alleviation. - Ask about factors that worsen the pain, such as certain activities or positions, and what provides relief. Functional Limitations: - Inquire about how hand pain affects activities of daily living, work-related tasks, hobbies, and personal care. - Identify specific tasks or activities that are challenging due to hand pain. Medical History: - Gather information about any previous hand injuries, surgeries, or medical conditions that may impact the patient's hand OA. - Consider any systemic conditions that might influence treatment planning. 2.

Objective Assessment: Observation: - Observe for visible signs of deformity, joint swelling, or enlargement of the distal and proximal interphalangeal (DIP and PIP) joints. - Note if there are any joint deviations, such as ulnar deviation or swan-neck deformity. Palpation: - Gently palpate each joint, assessing for tenderness, warmth, and swelling. - Note the presence of bony nodules (Heberden's and Bouchard's nodes) and any joint irregularities. Range of Motion (ROM): - Evaluate the active and passive range of motion of each finger joint, including flexion, extension, abduction, and adduction. - Pay attention to any limitations, discomfort, or pain during movement.

Strength Testing: - Use a handheld dynamometer or pinch gauge to measure grip strength, pinch strength, key pinch, and lateral pinch. - Compare strength between affected and unaffected hands. Functional Testing: - Observe the patient's ability to perform functional tasks such as writing, buttoning clothes, picking up small objects, and opening jars. - Note any difficulties, pain, or compensatory movements. Special Tests: - Perform specific tests to assess joint stability, ligament integrity, and functional capacity of the hand. - Consider tests such as the grind test to assess the integrity of the thumb CMC joint. 3.

Assessment of Functional Limitations: Functional Questionnaires: - Use validated questionnaires like the Michigan Hand Outcome Questionnaire (MHQ) or the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) questionnaire to quantify pain, stiffness, and physical function. 4. Communication and Education: Patient Education: - Communicate the assessment findings to the patient in a clear and compassionate manner. - Provide information about the role of physiotherapy, available treatment options, and the importance of adhering to the treatment plan. 5. Treatment Planning: Exercise Prescription: - Develop an individualized exercise program that includes range of motion exercises, strengthening exercises, and joint protection techniques.

- Focus on improving hand function, reducing pain, and maintaining joint mobility. Pain Management: - Discuss strategies for managing pain, including heat therapy, pain-relieving modalities, and ergonomic modifications. - Educate the patient about pacing activities and taking breaks to manage pain during daily tasks. Functional Training: - Integrate functional exercises that replicate the patient's daily activities to enhance hand dexterity and function. - Include activities that address fine motor skills and coordination. Home Exercise Program: - Provide the patient with a detailed home exercise program that includes instructions, frequency, and progression of exercises. - Encourage them to perform exercises regularly and document their progress. 6.

Regular Re-assessment: - Periodically re-evaluate the patient's progress to track improvements and modify the treatment plan as needed. - Collaborate with the patient and other healthcare professionals, such as occupational therapists, for a holistic approach to care.

## ASSESSMENT OF RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS (RA)

1. Subjective Assessment: Pain History: - Ask about the nature of pain: when it started, how it has progressed, and if it varies in intensity throughout the day. - Inquire about any factors that exacerbate or alleviate pain, such as rest, movement, or specific activities. Inflammatory Symptoms: - Explore the presence and duration of morning stiffness. Assess how long it takes for the stiffness to improve after waking up. - Ask about other inflammatory symptoms like joint swelling, warmth, and redness. Functional Limitations: - Discuss how RA affects the patient's daily activities, including self-care, work, and leisure activities. - Identify specific activities or tasks that are challenging due to pain, stiffness, or joint limitations.

Medical History: - Understand the patient's RA diagnosis, including disease duration, severity, and medications used. - Inquire about any past flares, systemic symptoms, or complications of RA. 2. Objective Assessment: Observation: - Observe for visible signs of joint deformities, such as swan-neck deformity, boutonniere deformity, and ulnar deviation. - Note any asymmetry, muscle atrophy, or joint enlargement. Palpation: - Gently palpate affected joints for signs of tenderness, warmth, and synovial thickening. - Identify specific joints with active inflammation and assess for nodules. Range of Motion (ROM): - Assess active and passive ROM of affected joints, focusing on the major joints involved in RA (hands, wrists, knees, and ankles).

- Evaluate for any restrictions, pain, or crepitus during movement. Muscle Strength Testing: - Perform muscle strength testing for specific muscles around affected joints. - Assess muscle weakness, which can contribute to functional limitations and altered movement patterns. Functional Testing: - Evaluate the patient's ability to perform functional tasks relevant to their daily life. - Observe for pain, limitations, compensatory movements, and difficulties during activities. Special Tests: - Use specialized tests to assess joint stability, ligament integrity, and functional capacity. - For example, assess the patient's ability to perform the finger opposition test to evaluate thumb function. 3.

Assessment of Functional Limitations: Health Assessment Questionnaires: - Utilize comprehensive questionnaires such as the Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ) to assess the patient's ability to perform daily activities and their overall quality of life. - HAQ considers various aspects like dressing, grooming, eating, and physical activities. 4. Communication and Education: Patient Education: - Clearly communicate the assessment findings to the patient and address any concerns they may have. - Discuss the collaborative approach involving physiotherapy, medical management, and lifestyle adjustments. 5.

Treatment Planning: Exercise Prescription: - Develop an individualized exercise program focusing on joint mobility, muscle strength, endurance, and cardiovascular fitness. - Tailor exercises to the patient's specific needs and address any joint limitations. Pain Management: - Discuss pain management strategies, including modalities such as heat, cold, and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS). - Educate the patient about pacing activities and the importance of avoiding overexertion during flares. Functional Training: - Integrate functional exercises that simulate the patient's daily activities to enhance their overall functional capacity. - Include joint protection techniques to minimize strain on affected joints.

Home Exercise Program: - Provide the patient with a comprehensive home exercise program, including clear instructions, frequency, and progressions. - Empower them to take control of their own care between physiotherapy sessions. 6. Regular Re-assessment: - Schedule regular follow-up assessments to monitor the patient's progress, adjust the treatment plan, and address any changes in their condition. - Collaborate with the patient's medical team, including rheumatologists and occupational therapists, for a holistic approach.

## ASSESSMENT OF ANKYLOSING SPONDYLITIS (AS)

1. Subjective Assessment: Pain History: - Inquire about the exact location, intensity, and nature of pain in different areas, such as the spine, hips, buttocks, and peripheral joints. - Understand how pain impacts daily activities, rest, and sleep. Morning Stiffness: - Determine the duration and severity of morning stiffness, and how it affects the patient's ability to function in the early hours. Functional Limitations: - Discuss how AS-related symptoms affect activities such as bending, lifting, walking, and maintaining posture. - Identify specific activities that are limited due to pain or stiffness.

Medical History: - Gather information about the patient's AS diagnosis, including disease duration, medications, treatments, and any systemic manifestations. - Understand any flares, complications, or hospitalizations related to AS. 2. Objective Assessment: Observation: - Observe the patient's posture, noting any changes in spinal alignment, curvature, or asymmetry. - Look for compensatory movements, such as leaning to one side or shifting weight. Spinal Mobility: - Assess spinal mobility using various methods, including the modified Schober's test to measure lumbar flexion and extension. - Evaluate lateral spinal flexion and rotation, noting any limitations or discomfort.

Palpation: - Gently palpate the spine, sacroiliac joints, and buttocks to identify areas of tenderness and inflammation. - Assess for any bony prominences, muscle tightness, or tender points. Functional Testing: - Evaluate the patient's ability to perform functional tasks like bending, reaching, and lifting. - Observe for pain, restrictions, and compensatory strategies during these movements. Special Tests: - Perform specific tests to assess joint mobility, spinal alignment, and functional capacity. - Use the occiput-to-wall test to assess upper thoracic extension and alignment. 3.

Assessment of Functional Limitations: Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI): - Administer the BASDAI questionnaire to quantify disease activity, including pain, stiffness, and fatigue. - This aids in assessing the impact of AS on the patient's daily life. 4. Communication and Education: Patient Education: - Clearly explain the assessment findings to the patient and address any misconceptions or concerns. - Emphasize the role of physiotherapy, self-management, and the importance of adhering to the treatment plan. 5. Treatment Planning: Exercise Prescription: - Develop a personalized exercise program emphasizing spinal mobility, posture correction, and muscle strengthening.

- Include range of motion exercises, stretching, and low-impact aerobic exercises. Pain Management: - Discuss strategies for managing pain and stiffness, such as the use of heat, cold, and pain-relieving modalities. - Teach the patient how to incorporate movement breaks to prevent stiffness. Posture Training: - Provide guidance on maintaining proper posture during various activities, including sitting, standing, and walking. - Emphasize the importance of ergonomic adjustments to minimize strain. Functional Training: - Incorporate functional exercises that mimic the patient's daily activities to enhance their overall function and confidence.

Home Exercise Program: - Provide a comprehensive home exercise program with clear instructions, frequency, and progression. - Encourage the patient to practice exercises regularly to maintain joint mobility and muscle strength. 6. Regular Re-assessment: - Schedule regular follow-up assessments to monitor progress and make necessary adjustments to the treatment plan. - Collaborate with other healthcare providers, such as rheumatologists and occupational therapists, for comprehensive care.

## ASSESSMENT OF GOUT

1. Initial Assessment: History Taking: - Inquire about the patient's occupation, hobbies, and activities to understand their physical demands and functional needs. - Discuss the patient's pain experience, including the nature, intensity, and triggers of pain. - Explore the patient's history of joint injuries, surgeries, or other musculoskeletal conditions that could affect their gout management. Physical Examination: - Perform a comprehensive joint assessment, including palpation, to identify areas of tenderness, swelling, and warmth. - Assess joint alignment and mechanics to identify any functional or structural issues contributing to gout symptoms. - Evaluate muscle strength and imbalances to design a personalized strengthening program.

Postural Assessment: - Analyze the patient's posture to identify any deviations that might contribute to joint stress and exacerbate gout symptoms. - Design postural correction exercises to alleviate strain on affected joints. Functional Movement Analysis: - Observe the patient's ability to perform functional movements such as walking, stair climbing, and squatting to identify limitations and areas for improvement. 2. Goal Setting: - Collaborate with the patient to establish short-term and long-term goals that are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound (SMART). - Prioritize goals based on the patient's preferences and needs, ensuring they align with their gout management plan. 3.

Treatment Approaches: Pain Management: - Use manual therapy techniques such as gentle joint mobilizations, soft tissue mobilization, and myofascial release to reduce pain and promote relaxation. - Incorporate pain education to help patients understand pain mechanisms and adopt coping strategies. Joint Mobility and Range of Motion: - Implement joint-specific mobilization techniques to address stiffness and improve joint mobility. - Teach the patient self-mobilization exercises to maintain joint function between sessions. Muscle Strengthening: - Develop an individualized exercise program focusing on strengthening muscles around the affected joints.

- Gradually progress from isometric to isotonic and eccentric exercises, taking care to avoid exacerbating pain. Proprioception and Balance Training: - Incorporate exercises that challenge proprioception and balance to enhance joint stability and prevent falls. Education and Lifestyle Modification: - Educate the patient about gout triggers, risk factors, and how lifestyle modifications can impact their condition. - Collaborate with a registered dietitian to provide dietary guidance that supports gout management. 4. Exercise Prescription: - Design a structured exercise routine that includes warm-up and cool-down components.

- Integrate aerobic exercises, such as stationary cycling or swimming, to improve cardiovascular fitness without placing excessive strain on joints. 5. Monitoring and Follow-up: - Regularly assess the patient's progress toward their goals using functional tests, joint range of motion measurements, and pain scales. - Adjust the exercise program based on the patient's response, and modify goals as needed. 6. Referral and Collaboration: - Maintain open communication with the patient's medical team, including rheumatologists and primary care physicians. - Collaborate with other healthcare providers to ensure coordinated care, especially if there are changes in the patient's medical management. 7.

Patient Education and Self-Management: - Teach the patient self-mobilization techniques, stretching exercises, and strengthening routines to promote independence and active self-management. - Provide resources on pain management strategies, lifestyle modifications, and the importance of adherence to the treatment plan. 8. Psychosocial Support: - Address any psychological factors like anxiety, depression, or fear avoidance that could impact the patient's engagement in physiotherapy and overall gout management. 9. Long-Term Maintenance: - Work with the patient to transition from active treatment to a maintenance program focused on sustaining gains achieved during the intervention phase.

## ASSESSMENT OF PERTHES DISEASE

1. Initial Assessment: History Taking: - Gather detailed information about the child's medical history, including any previous injuries, surgeries, or medical conditions. - Discuss any family history of hip or musculoskeletal disorders. - Ask about the child's pain experience, including its location, intensity, and aggravating factors. Physical Examination: - Thoroughly assess the child's hip joint, spine, and lower extremities for any deformities, muscle imbalances, or signs of hip instability. - Observe the child's posture, gait, and any compensatory movements due to hip pain or stiffness. - Evaluate for leg length discrepancy, as this can impact gait mechanics.

Hip Range of Motion Assessment: - Measure the child's hip range of motion, including flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, internal rotation, and external rotation. - Identify any limitations or restrictions in joint mobility. Muscle Strength Assessment: - Evaluate the strength of muscles around the hip joint, including the hip flexors, abductors, adductors, and extensors. - Assess strength imbalances between the affected and unaffected sides. Functional Assessment: - Observe the child's ability to perform functional activities, such as getting in and out of a chair, squatting, and walking up and down stairs. - Identify any challenges or compensations during these activities. 2.

Goal Setting: - Collaborate with the child, parents, and caregivers to establish specific goals for physiotherapy. These goals should be achievable and relevant to the child's age and functional needs. - Goals might include improving joint mobility, reducing pain, increasing muscle strength, and enhancing functional activities. 3. Treatment Approaches: Pain Management: - Implement pain-relieving modalities like ice, heat, or transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) during acute phases of pain. - Teach the child and parents techniques for pain management at home. Range of Motion Exercises: - Design a range of motion program that includes gentle stretching exercises to maintain hip joint mobility.

- Encourage regular, pain-free movement to prevent joint stiffness. Strengthening Exercises: - Develop a comprehensive strengthening program that targets muscles around the hip joint, core muscles, and the lower extremities. - Focus on progressive resistance exercises that are appropriate for the child's age and abilities. Functional Training: - Incorporate functional exercises that mimic activities the child needs to perform in daily life, such as squatting, stepping, and climbing stairs. - Adjust the intensity and complexity of these exercises based on the child's progress. Weight-Bearing Activities: - Gradually introduce weight-bearing activities as the child's pain allows, helping to promote bone health and joint function.

- Activities might include partial weight-bearing walking, standing, and gentle jumping. Balance and Proprioception Training: - Include exercises that challenge the child's balance and proprioception, such as single-leg stance variations and dynamic balance drills. - Enhancing balance can improve joint stability and prevent falls. Education and Home Program: - Educate the child and their parents on Perthes disease, its stages, and the role of physiotherapy in management. - Provide clear instructions for exercises to be performed at home and guidance on frequency and progression. 4. Monitoring and Follow-up: - Regularly reassess the child's hip range of motion, muscle strength, and functional abilities to track progress.

- Adjust the treatment plan as needed to address any changes or challenges that arise. 5. Collaboration and Referral: - Maintain open communication with the child's orthopedic specialist to ensure coordinated care and an integrated treatment approach. - Consult with other healthcare professionals, such as pediatricians or orthopedic surgeons, if needed. 6. Long-Term Management: - Continuously monitor the child's growth and development, addressing any issues that may arise during the healing process. - Modify the physiotherapy program as the child's needs evolve, and continue to support their physical well-being.

## ASSESSMENT OF PERIATHRITIC SHOULDER

1. Initial Assessment: History Taking: - Gather detailed information about the patient's medical history, including any previous shoulder injuries, surgeries, or medical conditions. - Inquire about the onset, duration, and nature of shoulder pain, as well as any aggravating or relieving factors. - Ask about any activities or movements that worsen or alleviate the pain. Physical Examination: - Examine the shoulder joint for any signs of inflammation, tenderness, swelling, or deformities. - Assess the patient's posture and neck alignment, as these can contribute to shoulder issues. - Evaluate the shoulder's range of motion, strength, and any muscle imbalances.

Shoulder Range of Motion Assessment: - Measure active and passive range of motion of the shoulder joint, including flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, internal rotation, and external rotation. - Identify any limitations, pain, or asymmetries in movement. Muscle Strength Assessment: - Evaluate the strength of key shoulder muscles, including the rotator cuff muscles (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis), deltoids, and scapular stabilizers. - Compare strength between the affected and unaffected sides. Functional Assessment: - Observe the patient's ability to perform functional activities such as reaching overhead, lifting objects, and putting on clothes.

- Identify any functional limitations, compensatory movements, or pain during these tasks. 2. Goal Setting: - Collaborate with the patient to set specific goals that are achievable and relevant to their condition. - Goals might include pain reduction, improving shoulder range of motion, regaining strength, and returning to daily activities. 3. Treatment Approaches: Pain Management: - Implement modalities such as ice or heat to manage pain and inflammation. - Provide manual techniques like soft tissue mobilization to alleviate muscle tension and improve blood flow. Range of Motion and Stretching: - Design a range of motion program that includes stretching exercises to address any joint stiffness and improve flexibility.

- Focus on gentle stretching of the shoulder muscles and surrounding tissues. Strengthening Exercises: - Develop a tailored strengthening program for the rotator cuff muscles and other shoulder stabilizers. - Incorporate progressive resistance exercises that gradually increase the load and challenge the muscles. Scapular Stabilization: - Integrate scapular stabilization exercises to enhance proper shoulder mechanics and prevent impingement. - Emphasize scapular retraction, depression, and upward rotation. Posture Correction: - Educate the patient about maintaining proper posture, particularly when sitting, standing, and working on electronic devices. - Provide exercises to improve postural awareness and strength.

Functional Training: - Incorporate functional exercises that mimic daily activities to ensure a smooth transition back to regular tasks. - Modify the exercises based on the patient's progress and comfort level. Education and Home Program: - Educate the patient on shoulder anatomy, the nature of their condition, and the importance of adherence to the physiotherapy program. - Provide instructions for exercises to be performed at home, emphasizing proper technique. 4. Monitoring and Follow-up: - Regularly reassess the patient's shoulder range of motion, strength, and functional abilities. - Adjust the treatment plan based on the patient's response and any changes in their condition. 5.

Collaboration and Referral: - Maintain communication with the patient's healthcare team, including orthopedic specialists or other relevant professionals. - Consider referral for imaging (X-rays, MRI) if necessary to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of the shoulder condition. 6. Long-Term Maintenance: - Guide the patient on how to maintain the gains achieved through physiotherapy. - Encourage them to continue with a home exercise program and engage in regular physical activity to support shoulder health.
